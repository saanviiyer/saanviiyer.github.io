About Me

✦   Hello! I'm Saanvi, an undergraduate at Caltech studying Computation and Neural Systems with a minor in Math and Information Science, advised by Markus Meister. Computation and Neural Systems draws on math, computer science, physics, and philosophy to study how brains and machines compute. That cross-disciplinary way of working started for me in the inaugural Integrated Core program, Caltech's honors sequence, which is where my interests took shape. Working across fields that usually stay in separate buildings got me hooked on problems that don't sit inside a single department.

What ties my work together is a single goal, reverse-engineering the brain, figuring out how neural circuits compute by reading them and acting on them. The concrete version I most want to spend my time on is closed-loop neuromodulation, using AI to stimulate neural circuits directly for clinical use. A closed-loop system reads neural activity, decides what to do with it in real time, and stimulates the right circuits back, so the treatment adapts to what the brain is actually doing instead of following a fixed script. For conditions where standard open-loop stimulation falls short, I think that kind of responsiveness is where the real clinical gains are.

Most of my research so far has gone into one half of that loop, reading the brain well enough to act on it. At Stanford Medicine I'm building a multimodal foundation model that pairs pediatric brain MRIs with EEG to locate epileptic lesions and seizure onset zones. In the Shimojo Psychophysics Lab I work on noninvasive stimulation with focused ultrasound, EEG, and fMRI, and design multisensory experiments to see how the brain responds. Before that, at Seattle Children's, I built machine-learning models tracing how medulloblastoma tumors grow out of developmental cell lineages, and in the Perona Lab I align 3D CT scans with patients' genomic data using contrastive learning. The data changes each time, but the underlying skill stays the same, turning messy biological signals into representations a model can reason over.

The stimulating half of the loop is what pulled me toward protein engineering. Optogenetics already does this with opsins, light-sensitive proteins that make chosen neurons fire when you shine light on them. Design a better opsin and you get finer control over which circuits you drive, down to specific cell types. It is a molecular route to the same goal I care about, reaching neurons directly instead of through the blunt reach of an electrode or an ultrasound field. So lately I have been reading my way into protein design as another angle on precise neural interfaces.

Outside my own research, I care a lot about pulling more people into science. I founded and led a SWENext chapter, mentoring students through hackathons and STEM outreach, and now I head Caltech's Neurotechers and Neuro/ML groups, where we're running the first Neurohackathon in partnership with the Allen Institute.
