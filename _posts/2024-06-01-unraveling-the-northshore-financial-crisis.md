Twenty-six million dollars. A mind-boggling sum to a young student. For reference, Michael B. Jordan's net worth is a measly $25 million.

For months, the news of our school district's crushing debt has circulated through the halls of Bothell High School. Students, teachers, and families have all felt the effects. With the elimination of loved programs like rowing and elementary school music education, there is real discontent with recent Northshore School District (NSD) decisions, yet few fully understand how deeply those decisions may affect students going forward. Amid the confusion and the lack of communication, one simple question keeps surfacing: why? Why did such a large debt accumulate, and how does the district decide which programs to cut? Answering it matters if we want to keep any more cherished programs off the chopping block.

The NSD's 2024-25 Budget Development webpage offers a partial answer, describing an anticipated deficit and listing contributing factors in order: declining enrollment, growing support programs, and the state's underfunding of public education. Another page names COVID as a central cause. The district's transparency is commendable, but there may be more beneath the surface. As one anonymous student put it, "I feel like [the NSD] is equivocating some of the information. All the writing is in third person and doesn't take responsibility, especially since so many loved programs are being eliminated."

One likely cause is regionalization. Crystal Rediske, a Bothell High School teacher who has attended district meetings for over fifteen years, believes it is the single biggest factor. As she describes it, "regionalization essentially means an unnecessary cost of salaries." Splitting the district into subsections multiplies administrative staff, each regional superintendent bringing their own assistants. "Our district is considered fairly small, so the regionalization is super unnecessary," she said. Public records list the top NSD deputy assistant superintendent salaries at $266,666, $244,083, and $231,805. "Our district was operating fine until Dr. Reid established regionalization," Rediske told me. "Once that happened... poof."

Ethan Nguyen, one of the district's student representatives, sees multiple causes at play. "Our district has an overspending problem," he said, describing how COVID grants only deepened the pattern of continually investing in new programs. When the state declined to provide additional funding, the district turned to budget and teacher cuts. Nguyen also pointed to enrollment: Washington funds districts through a prototypical school model tied to headcount, and "people here aren't having enough kids." Rediske agreed, noting that families are moving out as living costs rise and those who stay have fewer children. The district, Nguyen said, is losing about a hundred students a year. "The prototypical school model is honestly outdated. We are losing students, so we are losing money." Rediske added another wrinkle: families of students with learning differences told her that, feeling unsupported, they were choosing specialty schools instead, pulling still more students out.

So what now? It turns out the NSD has resolved most of the shortfall. "We've made a lot of progress toward closing the deficit; there's only $200,000 left," Nguyen said, meaning 99.2% of the debt has been recouped. The cuts did much of that work: eliminating the fifth-grade music program saved about $700,000 once instruments and transportation were counted, the rowing program cost $145,000, and the Woodmoor pool trimmed a large portion more. The rest came from quieter measures. Amber Kinder, head of the English department, described a district hiring freeze and rising class sizes, typical classes climbing from around 31 students toward 33 or more, with smaller classes eliminated entirely.

I asked Nguyen what students could do to prevent further losses. "Not much," he said. The future of the district is "very unpredictable," and a demographer the NSD hired projects the declining birth rate will continue for at least six more years. With only $200,000 left, the road to stability is still rocky, and programs like choir, the school resource officer, and some sports remain "on the bubble." Amid the uncertainty, one thing stays clear: the district needs more student involvement in its decisions. A more collaborative process, one that keeps students and staff genuinely in the loop, would produce choices that better reflect the people they affect, and might protect the programs worth keeping.
