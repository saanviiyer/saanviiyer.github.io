*Written for Hum/Phil 41 (Knowledge and Reality) at Caltech.*

As the evolutionary outlier in cognitive complexity, humans have long claimed superiority over the rest of the animal kingdom. We are not the strongest or fastest species, but our intelligence, creativity, and technologies have made us dominant. Other animals have brains, yet they seem to pale next to ours: we do mathematics, reflect on the past, and imagine realities that don't exist. In his *Discourse on the Method* (1637) and a later letter to Henry More, Descartes claimed that animals lack minds, resting the claim on two arguments: animals cannot communicate, and they cannot solve genuinely new problems. Since then, behavioral research has surfaced cognitive abilities well beyond the survival instincts once assumed to be the ceiling for animals. The question has fresh urgency in the age of AI. Is the human brain still paramount? I want to argue that animals do have minds, and that Descartes' claim cannot be easily accepted given evidence of consciousness, sophisticated communication, and problem-solving across the animal kingdom.

First, what Descartes actually meant. He allowed that animals display traits of intelligence, but insisted the mind is more than processing and reacting to sensory input. Crucially, "animals lack minds" is not a claim about intelligence or brainpower. It is a claim about consciousness. The Stanford Encyclopedia distinguishes two senses: *access* consciousness, the availability of a mental representation for use in reasoning and the control of action, and *phenomenal* consciousness, the qualitative, felt side of experience. When it comes to animals, phenomenal consciousness is the sense that matters most, the one most relevant to ethics and welfare and the one hardest to pin down.

Neuroscience gives us a way in. Consciousness is thought to arise from interactions between the neocortex and sub-cortical regions. In *The Human Advantage*, the neuroscientist Suzana Herculano-Houzel notes that humans have about 16 billion cortical neurons, against roughly 9 billion in gorillas and orangutans and 7 billion in chimpanzees, a difference of degree, not kind. In 2012, the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness held that birds show "a striking case of parallel evolution of consciousness," citing neural substrates for both sentience and cognition, including near human-like signatures in African grey parrots.

The parrot Alex is the vivid case. Over three decades, Irene Pepperberg showed that Alex could learn and use concepts of number, color, material, and shape, abilities the public compared to those of a four-year-old. Mimicry alone would not impress Descartes, but Alex's competence goes well past repetition. On communication more broadly, Descartes' point was that animals cannot form sentences, the smallest unit that expresses a complete thought. Yet we now see structured dolphin signature whistles and honeybee waggle dances that encode the direction and distance of food, the latter confirmed in recent entomology work at Virginia Tech. We have not found animal language that ranges over arbitrary concepts the way ours does, nor clear hierarchical grammar, so this evidence only partly answers Descartes. Problem-solving is easier to demonstrate. A 2024 Royal Society study of wild raccoons found that a quarter of them opened at least three novel doors over three months, trying different approaches, which reads as genuine flexibility.

The strongest objections deserve airing. On the "similarity" line, the philosopher Peter Carruthers argues that all animal behavior can be assimilated to the non-conscious activities of humans, acted on impulse rather than felt. On the "dissimilarity" line, a creature like the earthworm is so unlike us that extending human consciousness to it looks absurd, so perhaps we should not extend it to any animal with sharply limited capacities. But the first runs against evidence like Gordon Gallup's mirror self-recognition in chimpanzees, and the second is undercut by our simple inability to quantify how dissimilar an animal really is.

Whether animals have minds is a hard question precisely because the same evidence can be read more than one way, which is why consensus is elusive. Still, I come down on the side that animals do have minds and some measure of consciousness. From Darwin onward, all life shares a source, and it seems a hasty heuristic to imagine that only humans were handed the power of thought. As Herculano-Houzel puts it, our roughly 1.7-times advantage in cortical neurons over the runner-up species is "remarkable, but not extraordinary."
