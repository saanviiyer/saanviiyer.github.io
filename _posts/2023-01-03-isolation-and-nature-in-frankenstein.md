In early 2022, the World Health Organization reported that the COVID-19 pandemic triggered a 25% increase in the prevalence of anxiety and depression worldwide. Mandatory quarantines, however necessary, cut people off from friends, family, and community, and overall happiness fell with them. It is not hard to draw a line from that experience to Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein*. In her gothic novel, Shelley offers a cautionary picture of what isolation does to the mind. The story follows a man whose scientific passion curdles into obsession, and whose withdrawal from the people who love him pulls him into depression. Reading closely, Victor Frankenstein's shifting perception of nature reveals both the harm of isolation and the repair that comes from company.

When Victor is surrounded by the people he loves, he notices beauty, and that noticing reflects his inner state. Early on, after his creation kills his younger brother and an innocent girl is executed for the crime, Victor is crushed by guilt. He says he "shunned the face of man; all sound of joy or complacency was torture to me; solitude was my only consolation — deep, dark, death-like solitude." Yet he contradicts himself once he is back among his family in nature, admitting that "the sublime and magnificent scenes afforded me the greatest consolation that I was capable of receiving." The shift happens precisely when he is no longer alone. Later, traveling through Europe with his childhood friend Clerval, he lingers over the beauty of the cities and the "lovely Isis" spread into "a placid expanse of waters." His eye for beauty returns whenever his companions do.

The reverse holds too. Victor's blindness to nature marks the damage of his isolation. Across the two years he obsessively perfected his creature, he never once paused to notice the world around him. Only after Clerval nurses him back to health does he register what he lost, recalling that "the same feelings which made me neglect the scenes around me caused me also to forget those friends," and that his eyes "were insensible to the charms of nature." He had lived through two springs in that isolation and seen neither; he only feels the "divine spring" once a friend is at his side. The contrast between not seeing and seeing maps almost exactly onto the contrast between solitude and companionship.

Through how Victor perceives nature, Shelley quietly charts the opposing effects of isolation and connection on his mind. His descent into obsession, vengeance, and depression is a tragedy, but it is one readers can learn from. The themes still land today, as the world recovers from a pandemic and settles further into a digital age. We can do better with these feelings by taking our own mental health seriously and remembering that problems have solutions. As Matisse put it, "there are always flowers to those who want to see them."
