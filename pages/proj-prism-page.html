if a protein model's embedding moves the same way every time you apply a mutational process, does that mean the process changed the protein's biology?

Abstract

i expected a mutational process to leave a clean trace in a protein model, and the controls taught me the opposite. the lesson, to distrust a clean number until it clears its controls, is the one i carry into everything else. a mutational signature is the fingerprint a mutational process leaves on DNA, a specific pattern of base changes. a protein language model turns a protein sequence into a vector, and a lab can read a lot about a protein off that vector instead of measuring it. PRISM asks whether a mutational process, applied to the DNA that codes for a protein, moves that protein in a consistent direction through the model's vector space, which would be a readout of the functional change the process causes from sequence alone. across all 78 cancer single-base-substitution signatures, one signature, SBS17a, moves proteins toward the same family, PF17041, and ranks first of 78 in two independent groups of families, which looks like a real effect. the controls change the reading. a scramble that keeps amino-acid composition but destroys the signature's structure reproduces 72% of the movement, protein length alone explains 92.5% of how far proteins move, and a probe that names signatures at an AUC of 0.94 on familiar ones drops to 0.7% on signatures it never saw. the point is simple. a repeatable movement in a model's representation is a fact about the model, and it becomes a fact about biology only after it clears controls for composition, length, and transfer.

01 · The Challenge

why it matters where a model's embedding moves

most protein machine learning now runs through embeddings. a protein language model is trained on millions of natural protein sequences, and it learns to map any sequence to a vector, called an embedding, that packs in much of what the model knows about proteins. because that vector is so informative, labs increasingly read function, stability, and family membership off it rather than measuring those things in an experiment. that habit raises a tempting question. if you take a biological process that changes DNA, apply it to the gene for a protein, and watch where the protein's embedding moves, does the movement tell you what the process did to the protein?

a mutational signature is a clean way to ask this. a mutational process, such as a kind of DNA damage or a faulty repair pathway, leaves a characteristic pattern of base changes behind, and the COSMIC catalogue lists 78 of these single-base-substitution signatures. if applying one signature to a gene consistently pushes the protein toward a single family of proteins in the model's space, that would be a readout of functional consequence from sequence alone, with no experiment needed. if it does not, it is a warning that reading biology off the geometry of an embedding is easy to get wrong. the reason to care either way is that the same move, reading a conclusion off where an embedding sits, now sits under a lot of protein and drug design, and it is only as trustworthy as the controls behind it. PRISM runs that test and then runs the controls that decide which of the two it is.

02 · The Approach

the setup, before any result

i want to start with what the pipeline measures, in plain terms, because every claim later depends on it. a mutational signature gives, for each possible base change in its local three-letter context, how likely that change is under some process. for each signature i walk a protein-coding sequence and draw substitutions from those probabilities, which gives a set of mutants that the process would tend to produce. i translate each mutant into protein and embed both the wild type, the original protein, and the mutants with ESM-2, a protein language model that turns a sequence into a fixed vector. i then compare each cloud of mutant vectors against a library of Pfam family centroids, where Pfam is a catalogue of protein families and a centroid is the average vector of a family, so a shift toward a centroid means the mutants look more like that family to the model.

writing it with a little notation keeps the rest honest. for a signature and a starting protein, let the mutant embeddings be a cloud whose mean is m, and let the wild-type embedding be w. for a Pfam family with centroid c, the displacement is the reduction in cosine distance from the protein to c when i move from w to m, so a positive displacement means the signature moved the protein toward that family. cosine distance is just a measure of how different two vectors point, so reducing it means the mutant mean points more like the family than the wild type did. a second model, a recommender, reads the origin family, the target family, their difference, and the signature's profile, and predicts which signature produced a requested move. the trajectory work runs on ESM-2 8M against a library of 27,481 Pfam centroids.

03 · Results

three questions, asked in order

RQ1

one signature moves proteins toward one family, in two independent groups

the first question is whether any signature has a consistent destination. of the 78 signatures, SBS17a is the one that most moves sequences toward the Pfam family PF17041, the SasG_E domain, and it ranks first on that family in two independent Pfam clans, where a clan is a group of related families. I focused on the two-clan result because a direction that shows up once could be noise, while a direction that shows up in two unrelated groups of proteins is more likely to come from a real mechanism, so it is the one I put through the controls that follow.

Finding 1

SBS17a is the top-ranked signature of 78 for moving proteins toward PF17041, and it holds that rank in two independent clans.

RQ2

most of that movement is composition and length, not biology

a movement is only biological if it needs the signature at all, so i try to reproduce it without one. composition is the first thing to rule out. a protein is a string of amino acids, and the model's embedding depends heavily on which amino acids are present, so two proteins with the same makeup in a different order can land near each other in the model's space for no functional reason. i build a composition-matched scramble, which keeps the amino-acid makeup of the mutants but shuffles away the signature's specific structure, and i measure how much of the steering survives. the real SBS17a direction reduces cosine distance to the PF17041 centroid by 0.0425, the scrambled direction keeps 0.0306 of that, and the ratio is 72% over five seeds, so most of the movement needed only the composition.

length is the second thing to rule out. signatures differ in how often they create a stop codon, which ends translation early and makes a shorter protein, and shorter proteins move differently in the model's space for reasons that have nothing to do with the mutational process. so i fit how far each signature moves proteins against the length of the translated protein, one point per signature across all 78, and length alone explains 92.5% of the variation. between the two controls, the piece of the movement that is specific to the signature, rather than to composition or length, is small.

this is the part that matters most. if the plainest properties of a sequence reproduce an effect, the effect is not evidence about the mutational process, and calling it a functional finding is the mistake a reader makes by default when an embedding moves in a clean direction. the controls turn an interesting picture into a bounded claim.

Finding 2

a composition-matched control reproduces 72% of the steering and protein length explains 92.5% of the displacement, so the signature-specific residual is small and SBS17a is not a validated functional driver on this evidence.

RQ3

a probe can decode a signature without understanding it

the last question is about a different kind of claim. a natural way to argue a model understands a signature is to show that you can read the signature back out of its embedding, with a probe, which is a small classifier trained on the model's internal vectors. that argument is weaker than it sounds, and this is where it breaks. i train an inverse probe to read an embedding shift and name the signature that produced it, and on signatures it saw in training it reaches an AUC of 0.94, where AUC near 1 means the shifts are cleanly separable. then i hold out whole signatures and ask the probe to name mechanisms it never saw, and its top-1 accuracy falls to 0.7%, below the 1.3% a blind guess over the classes would give.

this matters because decodability is the evidence people cite most often for a model understanding a concept, and here it is fully present while the model has learned nothing that carries to a new case. a probe that works on familiar examples and fails on unseen ones has memorized a fingerprint, not a mechanism, and only the held-out test tells the two apart.

Finding 3

the probe reaches an AUC of 0.94 on seen signatures and 0.7% top-1 accuracy on held-out ones, so a decodable signal is not evidence of a portable understanding.

Limitations

PRISM measures geometry inside one protein model's representation, so every number here is a statement about the model and not about biological function. the trajectory results run on ESM-2 8M, and a 650M centroid library exists but covers too few clan-restricted families for a catalogue-wide comparison, so i could not test whether the results hold at a larger model scale. the audit is demonstrated on one framework and one signature-domain finding, and its generality to other frameworks and signatures is untested. there is no residue-level attribution yet, so nothing here isolates which positions carry whatever signature-specific residual survives the composition and length controls, and that is the experiment this points at next.
