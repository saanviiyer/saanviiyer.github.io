In one hand, I hold a 400°C burning blade. In the other, a gray wire connected to a coil, ready to unwind at my fingertips. When I look through my microscope, colorful specks reveal themselves to be an assortment of tiny black, white, and red electrical components. With my pair of trusty black-and-gold tweezers, I am set to assemble my circuit jigsaw.

Soldering fuses metal in extreme heat, instantly connecting electrical components together. When I solder, the delicate balance of power and responsibility rests at my fingertips––with one falter of the hand, I could end up severely burning myself. Despite this, I comfortably wield the soldering iron, but this wasn't how I started.

It wasn't just soldering that I shied away from. I used to be someone who stuck by the sidelines, believing they protected me from harm. In my sophomore year, I had the idea to create a community for girls interested in technology to boost female enrollment in STEM classes, but held back fearing I wouldn't be taken seriously. In Jazz Choir, I declined to perform any solos fearing potential embarrassment. So, when I began my internship at the University of Washington Sensor Systems lab in the summer after my sophomore year, I certainly feared the smoke-emitting blade-shaped soldering iron capable of liquifying solid metal.

Unsurprisingly, I stood on the sidelines and watched the lab members operate this dangerous tool. One lab member used it to build an acoustic levitator: suspending matter in the air against gravity using high-intensity sound waves. Next to her, another person was using the soldering iron to fuse a wireless charging pad for their earbuds. I slowly began to see this intimidating tool of destruction transform into an instrument of creation.

With curiosity pushing me past my initial apprehensions, I decided to try it myself. I started with a practice board and took a deep breath to calm my shaky hands. With my right hand operating the iron and my left holding the coil, I proceeded with laser-like concentration. After one second of contact with the iron, the wire began to melt. Immediately, I placed a resistor on the joint. It solidified. I stood with the iron raised as I looked at my board. Did I just do that?

Encouraged by my initial success, I started experimenting with discarded circuits to satiate my growing curiosity. What would happen if I swapped a resistor's position? Could connecting two capacitors double the energy storage? Every little creation from the soldering iron fueled more curiosity and paved the way for confidence that I could take charge and create something beautiful. My proudest creation from the lab is a renewable energy generator made through little circuits compounded together that can convert ambient radio waves emitted from cellular infrastructure into usable electricity. My prototype yielded 30 watts of power, just enough to light a large LED, yet I found the power of illumination in me far greater.

Overcoming my initial fear of the blazing soldering iron instilled in me a newfound sense of courage pushing me to venture out of the sidelines––a compounding effect extending beyond the workbench. When I returned to school after that summer, I possessed the confidence to start a school-wide SWENext club––a supportive network to encourage girls to overcome their apprehensions about pursuing technology careers by hosting beginner-friendly competitions, outreach events, and networking opportunities with female STEM leaders. This confidence also carried me through my first jazz solo, a Chet Baker classic, at the fall assembly where I finally sang my heart out.

I thank the soldering station for showing me that the other side of fear is full of possibilities, and that by fusing curiosity with purpose, I can create my own courage to take action. College will be a new experience, but I now have the confidence to embrace its opportunities and actively contribute to my community.
