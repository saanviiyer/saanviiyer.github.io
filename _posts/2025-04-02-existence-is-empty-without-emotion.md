The concept of *anomie*, coined by the French sociologist Émile Durkheim in the late 19th century, describes a breakdown of social norms, values, and expectations. As the criminologist Bruce DiCristina describes it, anomie names the broad feeling of being disconnected from society, a mix of aimlessness, disorientation, and moral confusion. Albert Camus explores this in his 1942 novel *L'Étranger (The Stranger)*, where the protagonist Meursault drifts through a sequence of events toward a murder and, eventually, the death penalty. Narrated by a passive man who seems guided by neither morals nor ambition nor purpose, the novel gives us a rare view into the mind and fate of someone hollowed out by an internal anomie. Against existentialism and the claim that life is inherently meaningless, Camus shows how a life without emotional connection collapses into isolation and purposelessness.

The novel opens with Meursault's flat reaction to his mother's death, and his detachment is immediate. Instead of grieving, he fixates on the heat, his hunger, the glare of the light. At the funeral he does not cry, and is irritated by those who do: "She sniffled a lot. Then finally she shut up... Now it was all these people not making a sound that was getting on my nerves." He smokes in the mortuary, refuses a last look at his mother's face with a single "No," and cannot even recall her age. From these early scenes, Camus establishes Meursault's anomie: he is alienated not just from society's expectations but from the emotional experiences that make us human.

That detachment infects his relationships. His romance with Marie is almost entirely physical; he describes wanting her in "that pretty red-and-white striped dress" but never her values or character. When she asks him to marry her, he says "it didn't make any difference to me," and when she asks why marry at all, he answers "it didn't really matter." Even marriage becomes an arbitrary impulse, drained of significance. With Raymond it is the same emptiness in a different key. Asked for advice about a supposedly unfaithful girlfriend, Meursault simply echoes back what Raymond wants to hear and nonchalantly endorses violence he has no evidence to justify. His disconnection does not only diminish his own life; it lets him do real harm to others.

The murder is the direct manifestation of that emptiness. Meursault narrates it as something happening *to* him, the sun's "scorching blade" slashing at his eyes, his whole being tensing around the revolver, as if he were acted upon rather than acting. Then he contradicts his own victimhood by firing four more times into a motionless body, "like knocking four quick times on the door of unhappiness." He registers, faintly, that something has happened, but never its moral weight. It is the dead end of a life lived without emotional connection.

His trial makes the point at the level of society. As the psychologist Saul McLeod notes, institutions reward those who appear socially adept and alienate those who cannot. Fittingly, the prosecutor barely dwells on the killing; he condemns Meursault for failing to mourn, telling the jury that "the day after his mother's death, this man was out swimming, starting up a dubious liaison, and going to the movies, a comedy, for laughs." The jury sentences him to death less for murder than for having "crime in his heart." At the end, Meursault "opens his eyes to the gentle indifference of the world," and in accepting it, finally reaches something like genuine connection.

Meursault's story reveals the paradox of existentialism: even if life has no inherent meaning, the absence of emotional connection creates an emptiness that renders existence hollow. It resonates now. The pandemic pushed billions into isolation and deepened our reliance on platforms that offer the illusion of connection instead of the real thing. Like Meursault fixated on sensation over feeling, we scroll through updates that stand in for human contact. As the writer Holly Dreger observes, social media promises to connect us across distance, yet used carelessly it can breed the same detachment as anomie. It may be impossible to grasp the meaning of life, if there is one, but simply *feeling* the emotions life carries is what makes all the difference.
