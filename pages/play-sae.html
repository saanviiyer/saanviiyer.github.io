Sparse autoencoders, live

Neural networks are tangled and messy, and each neuron usually responds to many unrelated things. A sparse autoencoder projects activations into a higher-dimensional space and forces only a few features to activate for each input. Still confused? Keep reading for a visualization of how SAEs work and to understand where they still fall short.

The explanations follow two guides, Adam Karvonen's intuitive explanation of sparse autoencoders and a gentle introduction to sparse autoencoders on LessWrong. Notes with a pink rule come from them. Your browser trains every autoencoder on this page, and every number in the text comes from those runs.

01

A language model turns each token into a long list of numbers at every layer. We call each list an activation. GPT-3 uses 12,288 numbers per token. My toy uses 3, so you can see each activation as a point.

The point on the right is one activation. Its three numbers give its coordinates on three axes, and each axis stands for one neuron.

02

Here I plot the activations for 600 different inputs. The model sees only these coordinates. Nothing in the data tells it what any point means.

03

I made this data, so I know what it holds. Each input contains one or two of 6 hidden concepts, which I label A to F. I gave each concept its own direction. An input that holds only concept A sits on A's line, and its distance from the center shows how much of A it holds.

$$\mathbf{x} = \sum_{i=1}^{6} w_i\,\mathbf{f}_i$$

The LessWrong guide writes activations in this form. Each \(\mathbf{f}_i\) is a feature vector of length 1. The weight \(w_i\) is the feature activation, which measures how much of that feature the input holds. The guide calls the meaning of \(\mathbf{f}_i\) its feature description.

04

Three dimensions fit at most three directions at right angles to each other. Six do not fit. In my toy every pair of concept directions meets at 63.4° or 116.6°, so any two of them overlap with \(|\cos\theta| = 0.447\).

The model stores more features than it has neurons and pays for it with overlap. Researchers call this superposition.

The LessWrong guide states the superposition hypothesis. Models learn feature vectors that are almost orthogonal, so many features fit in a small space. In high dimensions the number of nearly orthogonal directions grows exponentially (the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma).

05

Overlap does little harm when few concepts appear at once, a property called sparsity. Here, … of the inputs hold one concept and the rest hold two or more. As you scroll, the picture first lights the one-concept inputs, which sit on the six lines. Then it lights the others, which fill the thin fans between pairs of lines.

The LessWrong guide uses a coffee mug as an example. You describe a mug by its size and its use, and you need no astronomy, so most feature activations stay at zero. Karvonen notes that a fact such as one celebrity's birthplace can appear in fewer than one in a billion training tokens, and large models still learn it.

06

The direct way to read a network goes one neuron at a time, and superposition breaks it. A neuron measures the input along its own axis, so every concept direction that leans toward that axis moves it. As you scroll, the picture lights each neuron in turn. In my toy, … of the 6 concepts move each neuron, which makes every neuron polysemantic.

Karvonen describes one neuron in a language model that responded to academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests and Korean text.

07

A sparse autoencoder tries to find the hidden directions from the points alone. It has two parts. The encoder turns an activation into one score per feature. The decoder turns the scores back into an activation, \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\).

My autoencoder has 12 features for 3 neurons, so its middle layer is four times wider than its input. Each arrow shows one feature's decoder vector. I have not trained it yet, so the arrows point in random directions.

Karvonen explains why the extra width needs a rule. A layer this wide could copy its input and tell us nothing. A sparsity penalty keeps most scores at zero for each input. The LessWrong guide adds that the scores become the feature activations and the decoder vectors become the feature vectors.

08

Training adjusts the encoder and the decoder to lower one number, the loss.

$$\mathcal{L} = \lVert \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} \rVert^2 + \lambda \sum_{j} c_j$$

The first term measures the squared distance between an input and its reconstruction, the red line on the right. The second term adds up the feature scores \(c_j\) and multiplies the sum by \(\lambda\), the L1 coefficient. A larger \(\lambda\) pushes more scores to zero.

After each step I rescale every decoder vector to length 1. Without that rule, the model could shrink the scores and stretch the decoder vectors to dodge the penalty.

Karvonen points out that nothing in this loss asks for readable features. Readable features appear as a side effect of sparsity and accurate reconstruction.

09

Scroll to train. When you opened this page, your browser trained this autoencoder for 2,400 steps with \(\lambda = 0.4\) and batches of 32 inputs, and it saved a snapshot every 10 steps. Your scroll position picks the snapshot.

The arrows start at random and turn onto the concept lines. When an arrow comes within about 25° of a concept, I give it that concept's color.

10

Some arrows never settle. In this run, … of the 12 features fire on fewer than 1% of the inputs. Researchers call these dead features, and I draw them as pulsing dashed lines. They use compute and explain nothing.

The LessWrong guide reports that about 65% of the features in Anthropic's largest autoencoder were dead.

11

Follow one input through the trained autoencoder, and scroll slowly.

The encoder multiplies the activation by a matrix and adds a bias, which gives one score per feature. The ReLU function then sets every negative score to zero. Most scores end at zero, so the code is sparse.

The decoder multiplies the code by a second matrix. That product adds up the decoder vectors, each scaled by its score. On the right the scaled vectors join head to tail, and the last one ends at the reconstruction \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\).

Karvonen describes the whole model as a matrix, then a ReLU, then a matrix. On GPT-3 with an expansion factor of 4, the encoder maps 12,288 numbers to 49,152 scores, and researchers aim for fewer than 100 nonzero scores.

12

The last picture shows why people call decoder vectors features. Each time feature … scores above zero, the decoder adds a scaled copy of its arrow to the reconstruction. The lit points mark the inputs where that happens, brighter for higher scores.

Karvonen gives an example. Suppose GPT-3 stores "Golden Retriever" as one direction and decoder vector 317 learns that direction. Each time feature 317 turns on, the decoder adds a scaled Golden Retriever vector to the reconstruction. This works because multiplying a vector by a matrix takes a weighted sum of the matrix's rows.

13

Each feature has two vectors. The thick arrow shows the decoder vector, the direction the feature writes. The thin arrow shows the encoder vector. The encoder vector decides when the feature turns on, so it must also ignore the concepts that overlap with this one.

…

Karvonen reports that in real autoencoders the encoder and decoder vectors of a feature have a median cosine similarity of about 0.5.

14

The value of \(\lambda\) sets a trade-off. Your browser trained two more autoencoders on the same data, one with \(\lambda = 0.02\) and one with \(\lambda = 1.2\). As you scroll, the picture moves from 0.02 to 0.4 to 1.2.

With \(\lambda = 0.02\), about … features stay on per input and the reconstruction explains … of the variance. The arrows find only … of the 6 concepts. With \(\lambda = 1.2\), the arrows find … of 6, but the penalty shrinks every score, and the reconstruction explains only ….

Karvonen describes how researchers compare autoencoders. They plot L0, the mean number of nonzero scores, against loss recovered, which measures how much of the model's own loss survives when the reconstruction replaces the activation. My toy has no language model, so I report variance explained in place of loss recovered.

Karvonen now prefers BatchTopK autoencoders. A BatchTopK autoencoder keeps the k largest scores across a batch and sets the others to zero, so you set the sparsity directly with k and tune no penalty. The LessWrong guide mentions the related k-sparse autoencoders that OpenAI used.

15

On a real model nobody knows the true directions, so researchers name features from examples. The panel on the right lists the inputs that fire feature … most strongly, with the concepts that each input holds.

The LessWrong guide describes the method that Anthropic used. They ran a large set of text through the model, collected the passages where a feature scored highest, and wrote a short description that fit them, such as "food places". Karvonen calls the usual check a gut reaction, because natural language gives no ground truth.

My toy gives a ground truth, so I can check the name. Feature … points within 25° of concept ….

16

A name you read off examples might fit by coincidence. To test it, you change the activation yourself and watch what happens, a method called steering. A decoder vector has the same shape as an activation, so you can add it straight in.

Scroll to add more of feature …'s decoder vector to one input, up to 2.4 times its length. The point moves along the arrow. The panel shows every score before and after, and the score of feature … rises from … to ….

Karvonen describes the same test on Claude. When Anthropic added the Golden Gate Bridge feature to Claude's activations, Claude mentioned the bridge in every response.

17

My toy has 3 dimensions and 12 features, and I planted all 6 concepts myself. That is what the toy cannot show. Anthropic's largest autoencoder had about 34 million features, and a real model gives you no planted directions to check against.

Karvonen describes a chain of stand-ins. The training loss stands in for the proxy metrics. The metrics stand in for human judgments of which features read clearly. Those judgments stand in for the real goal, an understanding of how the model works.

The LessWrong guide lists open questions. Nobody has proved that models store concepts by superposition, and one argument says that autoencoders find combinations of features instead of single features. The guide also finds it surprising that a network full of nonlinear functions has activations that split so well into weighted sums.

Both guides still count sparse autoencoders as real progress. Karvonen argues that so simple an objective would not find readable features unless the model had learned something meaningful.

Further reading

The two guides this page follows, and the sparse autoencoder papers from my reading list that I return to most.

  • An intuitive explanation of sparse autoencoders for LLM interpretabilityAdam Karvonen, 2024.
  • A gentle introduction to sparse autoencodersLessWrong, 2024.
  • Are single-token sparse autoencoder features causally necessary?Cho et al., 2026. The authors remove 3.9M features one at a time across six models. Which features matter changes with the family of autoencoder used.
  • Rigorously assessing natural language explanations of neuronsHuang et al., 2023. Confident explanations of GPT-2 XL neurons still fail many observational and causal tests.
  • Obfuscated activations bypass LLM latent-space defensesBailey et al., 2024. Attacks reshape activations so that monitors, sparse autoencoders included, miss harmful behavior.
  • Quantifying feature space universality across large language models via sparse autoencodersLan et al., 2024. The feature spaces of autoencoders on different models look alike after a rotation.
  • Universal sparse autoencodersThasarathan et al., 2025. One dictionary of features shared by several vision models.
  • Transferring linear features across language models with model stitchingChen et al., 2025. Starting a large model's autoencoder from one moved over from a small model cuts training cost by about half.
  • Steering CLIP's vision transformer with sparse autoencodersJoseph et al., 2025. Feature steering applied to a vision model.
  • How visual representations map to language feature space in multimodal LLMsVenhoff et al., 2025. A language model's own autoencoders act as probes for image inputs.
  • Sparse autoencoders, again?Lu et al., 2025. A hybrid model with a proof that it recovers data spread over a union of manifolds. The data on this page has that shape.
