Since the dawn of time, humans have discovered and innovated. Technology has always been a primary driving force in the advancement of humanity, from the creation of the wheel to artificial intelligence. These developments have innumerable benefits and bring unprecedented convenience to complex and intensive human tasks. But how far is too far? The industrial growth of factories demonstrated the automation of human "doing." Artificial intelligence is now proving how much of human "thinking" can be automated too. It is important to understand and weigh the consequences before the very essence of what it means to be human is taken from us. In his article *More-Than-Human Science*, Brandon Boesch explores this and asks whether we should intentionally devote financial and intellectual resources toward an "automated scenario" powered by artificial superintelligence. Here I want to examine both sides and propose a resolution.

The promise of this technology is undeniable. AI can bring immense progress to computationally intensive processes, in science and beyond. Boesch notes that computer-driven systems are less time-consuming, less labor-intensive, and cheaper. He points to AlphaFold, the AI built to predict protein structure. Traditional methods like X-ray crystallography can cost up to $10,000 per structure and take a long time by hand, and DeepMind claims AlphaFold has saved "hundreds of millions of years" of research time. Having worked on a computational neuroscience project myself, I can attest that data aggregation and preprocessing eat a disproportionate share of the work. Tools that accelerate that part could make research, and many other computationally heavy processes, dramatically more efficient.

There is also a moral case. More promising than saving research time is the potential to save lives. AlphaFold is only the onset of what is coming. A self-directed system could design and run its own studies, replicating findings across thousands of datasets to reach conclusions that hold. It could launch millions of studies on rare tumors, cancers, and disorders, scraping through hundreds of thousands of datasets almost instantly, work that might otherwise take hundreds of scientists decades.

The reasons against are just as real. Giving artificial superintelligence too much independence invites unintended consequences. Boesch raises the darker possibilities: horrific weaponry, even "human enslavement or annihilation." Machines that calculate humans are depleting the planet's resources might conclude, coldly, that our removal optimizes the world. And there is a subtler loss. Curiosity is one of our most innate traits, the foundation of all discovery. If artificial superintelligence takes over science and learns things we cannot comprehend, science itself becomes disconnected from us. If something as human as science can be stripped away, what is next?

Despite this, I think we must invest in the technology, carefully, because the automated scenario is largely inevitable. We cannot fully block advancement, so it is more useful to make that advancement safe. The costs are not only existential: in 2020, data centers supporting AI used roughly 200 terawatt-hours of electricity, about 2% of global greenhouse-gas emissions, and that number will only rise. The solution I find most defensible is a human-supervised, AI-powered science, one that keeps human judgment in the loop while borrowing the computing power of the automated scenario. Drawing that boundary, deciding how much of science should run on humans versus machines, will be genuinely hard, but it is the work. We will need comprehensive legislation on ethical use, transparency and public education about the technology, and safeguards against mass job loss before this is woven fully into the real world.

Boesch writes, in the conditional, "if we are to remain human." The phrasing gestures at a doomsday. I am more optimistic. Humanity has persevered for hundreds of thousands of years, and I believe the advancements of science, however frightening, hold incredible potential to change the world for the better, so long as we build the protections against misuse alongside them.
