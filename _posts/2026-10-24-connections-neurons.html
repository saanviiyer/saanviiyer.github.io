TL;DR

I was inspired by a comment on X to test how LLMs play Connections! Grouping Connections words by representation similarity works well, as Qwen3.8-27B finds up to 1.12 of 4 groups per puzzle, compared to just 0.006 by chance. However, shared neurons do not drive this success. In fact, scoring groups by neurons that all four words share underperforms plain cosine similarity across every model size, and interpretable sparse autoencoder (SAE) features fare even worse. There does not appear to be a weather neuron. When I ask the models directly, they find fewer groups, and thinking before answering (or reasoning) improves the score but it still falls short. Finally, feeding the similarity solver's top candidates to a thinking model looked like the best approach at first, but a date split revealed that the model merely memorized puzzles published before its release date.

The idea

A Connections board contains 16 words with four hidden and scrambled groups of four. These groups range from straightforward (yellow) to extra-tricky (purple). Because language models store extensive word knowledge, I tried a shortcut. Instead of asking the model to solve the puzzle, I presented it each word in isolation, recorded which MLP neurons fired, and searched for four-word sets sharing the strongest active neurons. If the model had a dedicated "weather" neuron, the weather group should immediately jump out.

Reading each word individually was critical for my experiment. This is because the model never sees a word alongside its board context, and it cannot recall a memorized solution. Instead, it must rely entirely on what it already knows about that individual word.

The setup

For this experiment, I used every puzzle from June 2023 to October 2026, which resulted in 8,797 distinct words in total. I tuned all parameters on the first 200 puzzles, then scored the remaining 1,017 test puzzles once. For every word, I averaged activations across three short prompts (such as "A word: hail"). Next, I z-scored each neuron across the entire vocabulary so that a neuron only counted when it fired unusually hard for that word.

To score a candidate group, I identified the weakest activation among the four words for each neuron, which ensured that all four words activated it. Then, I averaged the top k neurons. An exact search algorithm chose the best four-way board partition, and an automated game engine simulated gameplay under standard four-mistake rules. Every competing method used this exact search and game engine, differing only in how it scored candidate groups.

Shared neurons easily beat random chance by over a hundredfold. Even so, standard cosine similarity performs much better. In practice, mean cosine similarity of the same MLP activations—or of the residual stream at the same layer—outscores shared neurons at every model size. Model scale only widens that gap: from 0.6B up to Qwen3.8-27B, residual cosine similarity climbs to 1.12, whereas shared neurons stall around 0.78 at 1.7B and fall to 0.74 at 27B. At the 27B scale, cosine similarity beats shared neurons by 0.38 groups per puzzle (95% CI 0.32 to 0.44).

My tuning experiments pointed to the same conclusion, that there existed no “weather” neuron. If dedicated category neurons existed, tracking just a handful would suffice to spot a group. But the shared-neuron score kept rising as I averaged across more neurons, climbing from 1 to 4 to 16, all the way up through 1,024, the highest value I tested. More telling, when I examined which neurons the four weather words shared most strongly, none tracked weather at all. In fact, the top shared neuron fired most intensely for STARRY, DOZEN, TUNGSTEN, and AURORA. Because raw MLP neurons are polysemantic, a group emerges only as a faint collective pattern across hundreds of units, never as a single dedicated cell.

Second try: interpretable features

This led me to an intuitive alternative. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) and transcoders separate activations into cleaner, single-concept features. To test them, I substituted Qwen-Scope SAEs and community transcoders in place of Qwen3-1.7B's raw neurons.

Surprisingly, performance dropped. Shared SAE features scored 0.33 groups per puzzle, and transcoders managed just 0.19, compared to 0.78 for raw neurons. To be fair, a single SAE feature carries far more signal than a single raw neuron (0.59 vs 0.32). However, SAE performance stops improving after averaging only about four features, whereas raw neurons continue to gain strength across hundreds.

Evaluating shared units averaged (k) on the 200 dev puzzles shows this contrast clearly. At k=1, SAEs lead with 0.59 groups, transcoders hit 0.43, and raw neurons trail at 0.32. At k=4, SAEs peak at 0.63, while raw neurons reach 0.53. By k=1,024, raw neurons climb to 1.13, while SAE features plateau at 0.55.

To investigate further, I checked feature selectivity directly by isolating narrow SAE features that fired for at most 1% of the vocabulary. As it turns out, the four words in a true group shared at least one such feature only 4.4% of the time. When present, it offered a powerful clue, since random distractors on the board shared one only 0.2% of the time. Some features looked remarkably clean, such as one that fired exclusively on RACECAR, MADAM, KAYAK, and MOM like a palindrome detector. Even so, they appeared far too rarely, and for wordplay-heavy purple groups, they showed up in only 0.5% of them. Adding them as bonus weights on top of cosine similarity shifted the score by just +0.015 groups per puzzle, well within the margin of error.

Third try: show it the whole board

Reading isolated words has an obvious flaw as the model interprets HEAT as weather unless the other 15 tiles point to the Miami Heat. To test whether context would help, I gave Qwen3-1.7B the shuffled board, had it read each word again on its own line, and extracted each word's activation after the model saw all 16 tiles.

Unfortunately, context made performance worse! Every method lost roughly 0.2 groups per puzzle; for instance, shared neurons dropped from 0.78 to 0.59. Showing the full board also raised contamination risks, so I split the test puzzles around Qwen3's release date. The drop was nearly identical across the 486 pre-release puzzles and the 531 post-release puzzles, confirming that memorization did not help. In short, context slightly blurred word boundaries, and the strict four-way split penalized those small errors heavily.

What if you just ask?

Next, I compared internal activations against direct prompting. I gave Qwen3-4B the entire board and asked it for the four groups, scoring solutions strictly with no reused tiles across 255 test puzzles split by release date.

Without thinking, the internal activations clearly know more than the model can articulate, as direct prompting finds barely half as many groups. Enabling thinking helps considerably (raising scores from 0.56 to 0.84), but it still trails the activation solver. On top of that, roughly one-third of the thinking runs hit the 8,192-token limit before finishing.

Since direct prompting and activation search solve different groups, I decided to combine them. My activation solver ranked all 1,820 possible groups, and I fed the top 16 candidates directly into the prompt of a thinking model. At first glance, this hybrid approach scored 1.06, comfortably beating thinking alone. It pulled roughly four out of every five answers from my candidate list, discovered 27 correct groups not on the list, and completed its reasoning within the token budget more frequently.

However, the date split exposed a major catch. The hybrid's entire advantage over the activation solver came from puzzles published before the model's release date (1.18 vs 0.98). On newer, unseen puzzles, the hybrid actually fell behind the activation solver (0.95 vs 1.05). These newer puzzles were not inherently harder, and in fact, my activation solver performed slightly better on them despite not being able to memorize text. Instead, feeding the model the full board alongside candidate hints simply allowed it to recognize puzzles from its training data. On unseen data, the plain activation solver performed best, and scale helped it most. Qwen3.8-27B found 1.29 groups per puzzle on newer puzzles, compared to just 0.95 for the 4B hybrid.

In full gameplay simulations, my activation solver won 14% of games using residual cosine on Qwen3-4B. By programmatically incorporating the game's "one away" feedback (discarding guesses that contradicted previous misses and prioritizing near-miss swaps) I raised that win rate to 25%.

Takeaways

First, a small model reading isolated words already finds a substantial share of groups (about a third of yellow groups and a tenth of purple ones), which beats the same model's direct guesses fourfold.

Second, the semantic signal lives in broad similarity across many units rather than in clean, isolated category neurons.

Third, the optimal layer consistently sits about one-quarter of the way through the network (Layer 7 of 28 on smaller models, Layer 10 of 36 on larger ones), demonstrating that early representations already capture word meaning.

Fourth, internal representations easily outperform generated words. Asking the model directly finds half as many groups without thinking, and even chain-of-thought reasoning fails to close the gap.

Fifth, the hybrid approach succeeded largely because it recognized training data, which means any full-board LLM evaluation requires a strict pre- and post-release split.

Finally, purple groups remain challenging, even for the model! Wordplay and anagrams/phrases require letter-level knowledge that is not captured in static word representations.

Caveats

Because the creators trained the SAE on Qwen3-1.7B-Base, I had to apply it to the post-trained model. On single-word prompts, both feature dictionaries leave 32% to 86% of activation variance unexplained; dictionaries trained directly on word-level activations might uncover more shared features.

Additionally, my "one away" gameplay logic uses a simple heuristic rather than an optimal game-theory solver, and direct-prompt runs evaluated a 255-puzzle sample (every fourth test puzzle).

Finally, I ran Qwen3-8B and Qwen3.8-27B on an A100 GPU while running the smaller models on a laptop. Because Qwen3.8 belongs to a newer generation, its 27B score reflects architectural changes alongside increased scale.

The community archive Eyefyre/NYT-Connections-Answers provided the puzzle dataset. I used Qwen3-0.6B, 1.7B, and 4B, alongside Qwen-Scope SAEs and mwhanna transcoders. I ran activation experiments on an M-series MacBook and GPU runs on a single A100.
