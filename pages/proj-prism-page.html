if a protein model's embedding moves the same way every time you apply a mutational process, does that mean the process changed the protein's biology?

Abstract

a mutational signature is the characteristic pattern of DNA base changes that a biological process leaves behind, and a protein language model maps a sequence to a vector. PRISM simulates all 78 cancer single-base-substitution signatures on protein-coding sequences, embeds the wild-type and mutant proteins with ESM-2, and measures how far each signature moves the mutants toward families of proteins in the model's representation. one signature, SBS17a, moves sequences toward the same family, PF17041, and ranks first of 78 in two independent groups of protein families, which looks like a real effect. the controls change the reading: a composition-matched scramble reproduces 72% of that movement, protein length explains 92.5% of how far sequences move, an unconstrained optimizer pushes a fitness score 5 standard deviations past the range of natural proteins, and a probe that identifies signatures at an AUC of 0.94 drops to 0.7% accuracy when it has to name signatures it never saw in training. the throughline is that a repeatable movement in a model's representation is a statement about the model, and it only becomes a statement about biology after it survives controls for composition, length, selection, and transfer.

01 · The Approach

the setup, before any result

i want to start with what the pipeline measures, in plain terms, because every claim later depends on it. a mutational signature gives, for each of the possible base changes in their local three-letter context, how likely that change is under some process. for each signature i walk a protein-coding sequence and draw substitutions from those probabilities, which gives a set of mutants that the process would tend to produce. i translate each mutant into protein and embed both the wild type and the mutants with ESM-2, a protein language model that turns a sequence into a fixed vector. i then compare each cloud of mutant vectors against a library of Pfam family centroids, where Pfam is a catalogue of protein families and a centroid is the average vector of a family, so a shift toward a centroid means the mutants look more like that family to the model.

writing it with a little notation keeps the rest honest. for a signature s and a starting protein, let the mutant embeddings be a cloud whose mean is m, and let the wild-type embedding be w. for a Pfam family f with centroid c, the displacement d(s, f) is the reduction in cosine distance from the protein to c when i move from w to m, so a positive d means the signature moved the protein toward family f. a second model, a recommender g, reads the origin centroid, the target centroid, their difference, and the signature's 96-channel profile, and predicts which signature produced a requested move. the trajectory work runs on ESM-2 8M against a library of 27,481 Pfam centroids, and the steering analysis reads hidden layer 5 of the same model.

02 · Results

four questions, asked in order

RQ1

one signature moves proteins toward one family, in two independent groups

the first question is whether any signature has a consistent destination. of the 78 signatures, SBS17a is the one that most moves sequences toward the Pfam family PF17041, the SasG_E domain, and it ranks first on that family in two independent Pfam clans, where a clan is a group of related families. the same direction showing up in two separate protein contexts is the kind of recurrence a real effect would produce rather than a one-off, so this is the observation the rest of the page tests.

Finding 1

SBS17a is the top-ranked signature of 78 for moving proteins toward PF17041, and it holds that rank in two independent clans.

RQ2

most of that movement is composition and length, not biology

a movement in embedding space is only biological if it is not already explained by the plainest properties of the sequence. i test two. first i build a composition-matched scramble, which keeps the amino-acid makeup of the mutants but destroys the signature's specific structure, and i measure how much of the steering it keeps. the real SBS17a direction reduces cosine distance to the PF17041 centroid by 0.0425, the scrambled direction keeps 0.0306 of that, and the ratio is 72% over five seeds. second i ask whether length alone predicts how far sequences move, because signatures differ in how often they introduce a stop codon and therefore in how long the translated protein is.

Finding 2

a composition-matched control reproduces 72% of the steering and protein length explains 92.5% of the displacement, so the signature-specific residual is small and SBS17a is not a validated functional driver on this evidence.

RQ3

the score looks healthy in bulk and goes blind where a design process would use it

a score is only useful for design if it stays right among the top candidates, because a search keeps the best-scoring sequences and ignores the rest. across a whole dataset the learned fitness proxy looks respectable, with a bulk Spearman of 0.53. among the top-ranked variants, the region a design process would actually act on, its utility falls to 0.15, and when i let an unconstrained optimizer select against it the optimizer pushes the proxy 5 standard deviations past the range spanned by natural proteins while producing sequences that are not credible proteins. a healthy average and a blind top tail are different facts about the same score.

Finding 3

the proxy's bulk Spearman of 0.53 drops to a top-k utility of 0.15, and an optimizer reward-hacks it 5 standard deviations past the natural-protein range, so bulk correlation does not survive selection.

RQ4

a probe can decode a signature without understanding it

the last question is whether a model that can name a signature has learned something portable about it. i train an inverse probe to read an embedding shift and name the signature that produced it. on signatures it saw in training it reaches an AUC of 0.94, which says the shifts are separable. when i hold out whole signatures and ask it to name mechanisms it never saw, its top-1 accuracy falls to 0.7%, below the 1.3% a uniform guess over the classes would give. decoding a signature from familiar examples is not the same as understanding the mechanism, and the gap between the two is the whole point.

Finding 4

the probe reaches an AUC of 0.94 on seen signatures and 0.7% top-1 accuracy on held-out ones, so a decodable signal is not evidence of a portable understanding.

Limitations

PRISM measures geometry inside one protein model's representation, so every number here is a statement about the model and not about biological function. the trajectory results run on ESM-2 8M, and a 650M centroid library exists but covers too few clan-restricted families for a catalogue-wide comparison, so i could not test whether the results hold at a larger model scale. the audit is demonstrated on one framework and one signature-domain finding, and its generality to other frameworks and signatures is untested. there is no residue-level attribution yet, so nothing here isolates which positions carry whatever signature-specific residual survives the composition and length controls, and that is the experiment this points at next.
